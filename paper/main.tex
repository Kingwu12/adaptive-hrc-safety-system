\begin{abstract}
Fixed protective zones can interrupt collaborative work even when a person is
stationary or moving away from the robot. This paper investigates whether context
can reduce that interruption without weakening a deterministic separation bound in
a human--robot ceiling-panel installation surrogate. The prototype compares a
fixed-zone reference, a reactive speed-aware envelope, and a predictive controller
combining task-phase recognition with short-horizon intrusion prediction. A
supervised three-state Gaussian-mixture hidden Markov model recognises approaching,
working, and retreating; a separate kinematic channel predicts a predefined rapid
movement toward the protected volume. The learned layer can reduce, but never
increase, the speed permitted by the geometric envelope, and a fixed inner boundary
retains priority. Thirty-two labelled development trials from three operators
yielded leave-one-participant-out offline phase accuracy of 0.821 and balanced
accuracy of 0.757; the causal online filter yielded 0.794 and 0.725, respectively.
These are development diagnostics, not evidence of safety or generalisation. A
counterbalanced within-participant experiment is specified using protective-distance
margin, intrusion response, end-to-end response time, false interruption burden,
and task time as primary outcomes. \pending{State the final sample and the
safety--efficiency controller comparison after collection.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Background}
\label{sec:introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{O}{verhead} panel installation combines material handling, alignment,
and fastening in a workspace shared by a worker and a large moving payload. The
worker contributes judgement and dexterity, while the robot can support the panel's
weight and repeat a constrained lift. This division of labour may reduce physical
demand, but it creates a coupled safety problem: the person's position changes
during the task, the payload enlarges the robot's occupied volume, and unnecessary
robot stops can disrupt coordination.

Industrial collaborative-operation standards describe speed and separation
monitoring (SSM) as maintaining protective separation and initiating protective
action before contact \cite{iso10218_2_2025,iso15066}. A fixed distance threshold is
simple and conservative, but it ignores whether the human is rapidly approaching,
standing still, moving laterally or retreating. Conversely, allowing an opaque
learned model to increase robot speed would make errors in activity recognition part
of the safety argument. This project therefore separates the safety-related
geometric bound from the learned context layer.

This paper therefore asks:
\begin{quote}
During one fixed ceiling-panel surrogate task, does a predictive SSM policy reduce
unnecessary robot interruption relative to fixed-zone and reactive speed-aware
references, without worsening response to a predefined rapid intrusion?
\end{quote}

The confirmatory claim is narrower than ``AI makes the robot safe.'' The study tests
whether the predictive policy reduces false interruption and task time while
preserving predefined response and separation outcomes. Developmental phase
accuracy is a mechanism check, not the primary safety outcome. The work also asks
whether objective effects agree with perceived safety, trust, and workload, without
allowing self-report to substitute for physical measurement.

\section{Literature Review and Critical Analysis}
\subsection{Construction HRC}
Robotic construction research has increasingly moved from isolated automation
toward systems in which human expertise and robot strength are combined
\cite{bock2015construction,zhang2023construction}. Reviews of construction HRC
identify safety, perception, communication and task allocation as continuing
barriers to deployment \cite{wei2023construction,lin2025safety}. Panel installation
is an appropriate case study because the person and robot must coordinate around a
shared object and because excessive conservative stopping directly affects task
fluency.

\subsection{Speed and Separation Monitoring}
SSM requires more than drawing a circle around a robot. The required separation
depends on human and robot motion during sensing, controller reaction and robot
stopping, together with intrusion and position-uncertainty allowances
\cite{marvel2017implementing,iso15066}. Dynamic approaches can update the
protective distance or command as motion changes \cite{karagiannis2022adaptive,
byner2019dynamic}. The controller developed here is standards-informed but is not a
certified SSM function: its simplified equation and software output must not be
confused with validation of the installed robot, tool, payload, sensing uncertainty
or safety-rated stop chain.

The literature exposes a practical tension. Worst-case assumptions are transparent
but can create nuisance stops, whereas adaptive methods can improve availability at
the cost of more assumptions about tracking, prediction, and stopping behaviour.
Published controller comparisons do not always separate the benefit of measured
closing speed from the benefit of learned context. They also commonly report
minimum distance or cycle time without tracing the complete chain from sensed
motion to robot deceleration. This makes apparently similar ``adaptive SSM''
results difficult to compare and motivates the three-rung decomposition used here.

\subsection{Human Activity Recognition and Collaboration}
Hidden Markov models (HMMs) provide an interpretable probabilistic representation
of temporal state. They describe a hidden state sequence through a transition
matrix and relate each state to observable measurements through an emission model
\cite{rabiner1989}. HMMs have been used in wearable activity recognition and
human--robot task modelling \cite{manouchehri2023hmm,berg2018assembly}. However,
task phase and hazard are different variables: a person can trip while approaching,
working or retreating. This project therefore uses the HMM only for task context and
uses an independent geometric predictor for the operational rapid-intrusion event.

This distinction addresses an inconsistency in activity-based safety framing.
Semantic states such as ``working'' or ``retreating'' describe what normally occurs,
whereas a hazardous situation is defined by exposure to harm. Treating hazard as a
mutually exclusive activity label can hide a dangerous movement inside an otherwise
normal phase and confound state duration with event recognition. Separating these
variables also makes model failure less consequential: the deterministic envelope
remains observable and testable without trusting the HMM's interpretation.

Objective collaboration fluency is commonly evaluated through temporal measures
such as human idle time, robot idle time, concurrent activity and functional delay
\cite{hoffman2019fluency}. Perceived workload and trust can add explanatory
evidence, but subjective reports cannot establish physical safety or replace
measured response \cite{hart1988tlx,jian2000trust}.

Taken together, prior work leaves three gaps addressed by this study: controlled
attribution across fixed, reactive, and predictive policies; an architecture in
which learned context cannot relax the deterministic bound; and an evaluation that
combines response-chain measurements, interruption burden, task performance,
tracking integrity, and secondary human judgements. The experiment is limited to
one robot, one surrogate task, and one defined intrusion, so it tests these
propositions in a bounded setting rather than claiming universal hazard recognition.

\section{Research Question and Hypotheses}
\label{sec:question}
The independent variable is controller condition: fixed zone, reactive envelope,
or predictive envelope. The primary estimands are paired, participant-level
differences in intrusion response, protective-distance margin, false interruption
burden, and task time. The hypotheses are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{H1 (response non-inferiority):} the predictive condition does not
worsen rapid-intrusion response or protective-distance margin relative to either
reference within a predeclared engineering margin.
\item \textbf{H2 (specificity):} the predictive condition reduces false stops and
slowdown duration during clean and non-intrusion distractor windows.
\item \textbf{H3 (efficiency):} the predictive condition reduces task time and idle
time without increasing task errors or rework.
\end{itemize}

The non-inferiority margin in H1 must be fixed from validated sensing and stopping
uncertainty before analysis; failure to reject a difference is not evidence of
equivalence. Subjective outcomes are secondary and non-directional because greater
monitoring or lower perceived safety may reflect vigilance, novelty, task demand,
or distrust.

\subsection{Physical and Sensing Architecture}
The experimental cell uses a UR10 robot holding a lightweight ceiling-panel
surrogate. The primary online human-position stream is an OptiTrack rigid body
mounted on the participant's head. The robot tool-centre point (TCP) is refreshed
from live robot telemetry on every control tick. The software provides a concurrent
23-segment Xsens MVN recording path for segment positions and quaternions, but
Xsens is not an input to the frozen phase classifier. The complete recording path
must still pass the pre-study qualification gate. This distinction prevents the
presence of a full-body suit from being misreported as a model feature or as
already verified data.

The controller operates at a nominal 60~Hz. Each accepted trial is intended to
retain: (i) the dashboard JSONL stream, (ii) all received Xsens segments,
(iii) raw OptiTrack rigid-body poses and tracking quality, (iv) robot telemetry,
(v) a native MVN recording, (vi) a native Motive take, (vii) a synchronized video,
and (viii) an event journal. All artifacts share a participant and trial identifier.
Missing or stale tracking invokes a conservative stop request and invalidates a
reported trial under the frozen exclusion rule.

\subsection{Geometric Features}
The online feature extractor presently measures the distance from the tracked head
proxy to the nearest point on a vertical occupied-column proxy located at the live
TCP's horizontal coordinates. For participant position $\mathbf{p}$ and the nearest
point $\mathbf{q}$ on this proxy,
\begin{equation}
d(t)=\lVert\mathbf{q}(t)-\mathbf{p}(t)\rVert_2 .
\end{equation}
A least-squares slope over recent position samples estimates velocity
$\mathbf{v}$. With unit direction $\mathbf{u}$ from the participant toward the
proxy, closing speed is
\begin{equation}
v_{\mathrm{proj}}(t)=\mathbf{v}(t)^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{u}(t),
\end{equation}
where positive values indicate closing motion. Speed magnitude, projected
acceleration and velocity-heading alignment are derived from the same window.

The current vertical column is an engineering proxy, not final protective
geometry. It must be replaced or conservatively validated against the complete
robot, tool, panel and swept volume before protective-distance results are
reportable. In particular, one head point cannot prove hand or limb clearance.

\subsection{Task-Phase GMM-HMM}
The learning target contains three mutually exclusive phases:
\emph{approaching}, \emph{working} and \emph{retreating}. The observation vector is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_t=[d_t,\;v_{\mathrm{proj},t},\;\lVert\mathbf{v}_t\rVert,\;
h_t]^\mathsf{T},
\end{equation}
where $h_t$ is velocity-heading alignment with the direction toward the protected
proxy. Despite a legacy software field name, $h_t$ is not torso orientation.

For state $z_t$, the emission probability is a three-component diagonal Gaussian
mixture,
\begin{equation}
p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid z_t=i)=\sum_{k=1}^{3}w_{ik}
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_t;\boldsymbol{\mu}_{ik},
\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{ik}^{2})).
\end{equation}
The transition matrix is estimated from labelled phase sequences with Laplace
smoothing. Long contiguous phases made the raw matrix highly persistent; the
configured transition-power transformation further sharpens that persistence.
Mixture parameters are fitted by expectation--maximization to labelled samples
within each phase. Online inference uses the causal forward recursion
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t \propto
p(\mathbf{x}_t\mid z_t)\odot
(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{A}),
\end{equation}
whereas offline validation also reports Viterbi decoding over the full sequence.
The offline and causal estimates are not interchangeable.

\subsection{Independent Rapid-Intrusion Prediction}
A hazard is not a state label. For this study, the operational event is an
experimenter-cued, approved rapid movement of the tracked proxy toward the
validated protected volume. A constant-acceleration extrapolation estimates time
to the fixed inner boundary by solving
\begin{equation}
d(t)-r_{\mathrm{red}}-v_{\mathrm{proj}}\tau
-\tfrac{1}{2}a_{\mathrm{proj}}\tau^2=0
\end{equation}
for the smallest non-negative $\tau$ within a 0.5-s horizon. Projected acceleration
is clamped to suppress implausible motion-capture spikes, and the event channel is
gated on genuine closing motion. It anticipates one predefined boundary-intrusion
event; it does not infer intent, diagnose a trip or claim coverage of all hazards.

\subsection{Three-Rung Controller and Software Shield}
The experimental controller ladder isolates what each ingredient contributes.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Fixed zone:} a constant worst-case protective threshold maps green,
yellow and red bands directly to full speed, reduced speed and stop request.
\item \textbf{Reactive envelope:} a simplified dynamic envelope uses measured
closing speed but no learned state or horizon prediction.
\item \textbf{Predictive envelope:} the same envelope is combined with HMM
task-phase caution and rapid-intrusion horizon prediction.
\end{enumerate}

The simplified dynamic separation used by the two envelope controllers is
\begin{equation}
S(t)=\max(0,v_{\mathrm{proj}}(t))T+C+S_a ,
\label{eq:envelope}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the combined reaction and stopping-time parameter, $C$ is intrusion
allowance and $S_a$ is position uncertainty. A ramp above $S(t)$ maps separation
to an envelope-permitted speed fraction $u_{\mathrm{env}}\in[0,1]$. This is a
research comparator rather than the complete ISO protective-separation equation.

The adaptive command is constrained by
\begin{equation}
u_{\mathrm{cmd}}(t)=\min\{u_{\mathrm{env}}(t),u_{\mathrm{context}}(t)\}.
\label{eq:shield}
\end{equation}
The context layer can therefore add a slowdown but cannot authorize a speed above
the deterministic bound. A fixed red-boundary check has priority over both layers,
and sustained imminent-intrusion risk can request an earlier stop. Unit tests lock
the inequality in (\ref{eq:shield}) and the red-boundary override. These are useful
software invariants, but they are not evidence of a safety-rated output.

\subsection{Robot Output and Operator Console}
The live research harness maps the speed fraction to the UR speed slider and maps
a stop request to Dashboard pause with a zero speed fraction. Dashboard pause is
not a safety-rated protective stop. A reported moving-participant comparison
therefore requires an independently witnessed safety-rated protective path,
measured end-to-end response and approved operating procedure.

The operator dashboard consolidates participant registration, block and event
assignment, live Xsens and OptiTrack status, model identity, phase and event
labelling, robot/panel actions, run history and QR hand-offs to the questionnaires.
It rejects duplicate trial identifiers and incomplete 23-segment Xsens frames for
recorded runs. The dashboard is an operator and traceability interface; its
availability does not itself close physical safety gates.

\section{Model Development}
\subsection{Development Data and Labelling}
The active model artifact contains 32 accepted labelled trials from three
development operators: eight trials from P03 and twelve each from P04 and P05.
Across the trials, 17,267 frames were labelled \emph{approaching}, 53,609
\emph{working} and 19,611 \emph{retreating}. Trials with incomplete required
phase sequences or invalid gaps were excluded from fitting according to the
development pipeline. The imbalance illustrates why overall frame accuracy alone
is insufficient: a classifier can benefit disproportionately from the longer
working periods.

\subsection{Validation}
Leave-one-participant-out validation fits the model to two operators and evaluates
the held-out operator, repeating this for all three participants. This is more
defensible than a random frame split because adjacent 60-Hz frames from one trial
are strongly correlated. Nevertheless, the same folds informed model development,
so the estimate is not an untouched final test.

\section{Participant Study Method}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Design}
The final experiment is a within-participant comparison of the three controllers.
Each participant completes three blinded blocks labelled A, B and C. Controller
order is counterbalanced, and the participant is not told which controller is
active. Each block contains three trials: one clean trial, one predefined
non-intrusion distractor and one approved rapid-intrusion trial. Event order is
rotated within blocks. Thus each participant completes nine trials, and every
controller is observed once under each event class.

The participant, rather than each 60-Hz frame, is the independent unit for
controller-effect inference. Rapid-intrusion events are the units for response
estimates. The model, thresholds, event definition, geometry, exclusion rules and
analysis code are frozen before the first reported participant.

\subsection{Task and Procedure}
At the beginning of a session the researcher explains the task, obtains consent,
fits and calibrates Xsens, confirms OptiTrack identity and runs a shared
synchronization event. The participant starts at a marked position carrying the
lightweight panel. With the arm in the verified low pose, the participant approaches
the gripper and aligns the panel with both suction cups. After grip verification,
the participant retreats to the start marker before the robot lifts the panel.
The participant then approaches the raised panel and performs the prescribed panel
task. During this work window the scheduled clean, distractor or rapid-intrusion
condition occurs. The participant subsequently retreats, the robot lowers the
panel, and the participant supports it before suction is released. Robot path,
panel, stance marks, wording and stopping policy are held constant across
controllers.

After each block, the participant scans the dashboard QR code and completes a
blinded block questionnaire on their phone. A final QR code opens the comparison
questionnaire after all three blocks. The researcher confirms that each response
is linked by participant identifier and block label without exposing controller
identity.

\subsection{Outcome Measures}
The primary outcomes are objective:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Intrusion response success:} whether the required command occurs
before the validated boundary would be crossed.
\item \textbf{Protective-distance margin:} the minimum of
$d_{\mathrm{validated}}(t)-S_{\mathrm{required}}(t)$ and duration below zero.
\item \textbf{End-to-end response time:} from measured movement or boundary
evidence through controller command and robot deceleration onset to verified zero
motion.
\item \textbf{False interruption burden:} false stops per safe minute and total
stop/slowdown duration in clean and distractor windows.
\item \textbf{Task time:} frozen task-start to task-complete duration.
\end{enumerate}

Secondary outcomes include anticipation lead time, minimum separation, integrated
speed deficit, human idle time, robot idle time, concurrent activity, functional
delay, task errors or rework, and tracking-valid proportion. Perceived safety,
trust and NASA-TLX workload are secondary self-report outcomes
\cite{hart1988tlx,jian2000trust}.

Whole-trial counts of 'looking at the robot' are not used because the task itself
encourages continuous observation. An exploratory head-directed monitoring proxy
is computed only in a predefined autonomous-motion window: proportion directed
toward the robot, monitoring revisits, first-turn latency and longest off-robot
interval. This is head direction, not eye gaze or trust. A predeclared video subset
is double-coded to estimate agreement and identify ambiguous or missing intervals.

\subsection{Analysis Plan}
Each measure is first summarized within trial and then within controller for each
participant. Participant-level paired differences are reported with confidence
intervals and effect sizes. For three-condition comparisons with a small sample,
a Friedman omnibus test is planned, followed only when justified by paired
Wilcoxon signed-rank or exact paired-permutation comparisons with Holm correction.
Intrusion response is reported using event counts and binomial uncertainty.
No inference treats adjacent frames as independent participants.

For H1, the engineering non-inferiority margin is fixed before unblinding and the
paired confidence interval is compared with that margin. If the achieved sample is
too small for the planned inferential procedure, effect estimates and uncertainty
are reported descriptively and the confirmatory claim is withheld. Missingness,
tracking failures, protocol deviations, and exclusions are tabulated by condition;
no participant or trial is excluded because its result is inconvenient.

\section{Discussion}
The development evidence supports a narrow conclusion: the implemented GMM-HMM
can recover useful task-phase context in this cell, but causal performance is not
uniform across phases and has not yet been confirmed on an untouched participant.
It does not support a claim of general hazard detection. That claim is unnecessary
for the controller architecture because the rapid-intrusion channel is independent,
and because the learned phase layer cannot command a speed above the envelope.

The final interpretation will be based on the joint safety--efficiency result.
A reduction in task time alone is not beneficial if protective-distance margin or
intrusion response worsens. Conversely, a controller that always stops may appear
safe on a small event set while imposing unacceptable interruption. Clean and
distractor trials are therefore essential for measuring specificity and false
interruption, not optional demonstrations.

\pending{Interpret the participant-level controller contrasts and uncertainty
without converting null results into equivalence claims.}

The discussion will follow four result-dependent decision paths. If predictive SSM
improves efficiency while satisfying H1, the evidence supports bounded usefulness
for this task and apparatus only. If efficiency improves but response worsens, the
adaptive claim fails. If response is preserved but efficiency does not improve, the
learned layer has not demonstrated practical value over the simpler envelope. If
objective and subjective measures diverge, the divergence is treated as a human-
factors result rather than selecting the more favourable measure.

Bias can enter through convenience sampling, operator-paced robot actions,
participant learning and fatigue, visible robot behaviour, motion-capture dropout,
body-size-dependent marker geometry, and experimenter-cued events. Counterbalanced
order, fixed wording, blinded condition labels, synchronized logs, participant-level
analysis, retained failed trials, and explicit tracking-quality measures reduce but
do not eliminate these sources. The final discussion must compare the observed
pattern with the specific mechanisms identified in Section~II rather than merely
restating significance tests.

\section{Contribution to the Field}
The contribution is a testable decomposition of adaptive SSM rather than a claim
that activity recognition is itself a safety function. The prototype separates
(i) measured closing kinematics, (ii) learned task context, (iii) independent
intrusion prediction, and (iv) a deterministic command ceiling. This permits the
experiment to attribute any reduction in interruption to reactive or predictive
information while retaining a common software constraint across the envelope
conditions.

A second contribution is the measurement contract: controller performance is
evaluated through validated separation geometry, end-to-end response, false
interruption burden, task fluency, data integrity, and secondary participant
judgement. If the final evidence supports the hypotheses, the demonstrable value is
a bounded safety--efficiency improvement for the ceiling-panel surrogate. If it
does not, the same design identifies whether the limiting factor is learned
context, the response chain, sensing integrity, or task-level usefulness.

\section{Safety, Ethics and Data Integrity}
The study distinguishes a hazard source, an initiating event, a hazardous
situation and possible harm. For example, a trip may initiate movement toward the
robot, but the controlled variable is intrusion into the moving robot--tool--panel
volume. Participants must not be asked to perform uncontrolled falls. The test
event is a scripted movement defined within the approved protocol, with a
lightweight surrogate panel, supervision, reachable emergency stop and bounded
robot motion.

Before reported collection, the installed system must have witnessed evidence for
reaction and stopping time, sensing uncertainty, protected geometry, safety-rated
output, controller integration, synchronized objective logging, participant data
flow, protocol reconciliation and a frozen analysis plan. Trials are never
overwritten or deleted; aborted and excluded runs retain their reason. A run with
missing native recordings, failed synchronization, stale sensing or incomplete
telemetry is preserved but not silently counted as complete.

\section{Limitations}
The present geometric input is a single OptiTrack head proxy, while hand or torso
intrusion may occur without equivalent head motion. Full-body Xsens is retained for
secondary analysis but has not yet been validated as an online protective sensor.
The vertical occupied-column model is simpler than the robot's true swept volume.
The dynamic envelope omits terms required by a complete installed SSM calculation,
and the current Dashboard pause path is not safety rated. Model development used
only three operators, creating limited evidence about between-person
generalization. Finally, the operational rapid intrusion covers one controlled
event and cannot represent arbitrary trips, falls, tool failures, payload loss or
unexpected robot motion. These hazards require independent engineering controls.
The convenience sample may not represent construction workers, and repeated blocks
may induce learning or fatigue despite counterbalancing. The event is cued and
controlled rather than an authentic loss of balance. The sample size will constrain
precision, particularly for uncommon failures; absence of an observed incident
cannot establish safety. Finally, the study evaluates a research prototype, not a
certified industrial safety system.

\section{Conclusion}
This project implements a context-aware HRC research platform in which task-phase
recognition, rapid-intrusion prediction and deterministic motion constraints are
kept conceptually and computationally separate. The three-state GMM-HMM provides
useful development performance, but its causal balanced accuracy and small
participant pool preclude broad generalization. The key experimental contribution
is therefore not an 'AI safety' claim; it is a controlled comparison of whether
