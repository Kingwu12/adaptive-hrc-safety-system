\begin{abstract}
In ceiling-panel installation, a worker and a robot share a workspace while
handling the same panel. Fixed safety zones cannot tell whether the worker is
approaching, working or moving away, so they can stop the robot more often than
needed. This paper presents a speed and separation monitoring (SSM) controller
that uses the task phase, and tests it on a physical prototype with
\PubPeople{} participants. A hidden Markov model (HMM) estimates the task phase
from motion-capture data, a separate predictor detects fast movement toward the
robot, and a fixed rule ensures that neither learned part can make the robot
faster than the basic distance-based limit allows. Because the system logged
every input, we replayed all three controllers (fixed zone, reactive and
predictive) on the identical recorded movement of all \PubTrials{} trials, and
the replay matched every logged robot command. On the same movement, every
controller had requested a stop at all \PubEntries{} entries into the red
boundary. The predictive controller started stopping \PubLeadPF{}~s earlier
than fixed-zone control and added \PubUnnecPF{}~s per minute of unnecessary
stopping, consistently for all \PubPeople{} people. Its early stopping matters
when the distance measurement is wrong: with a 5-cm distance error, a stop had
already been requested at \PubCovPredFive\% of entries under predictive
control, against \PubCovFixedFive\% under fixed-zone control. The robot
reached standstill a median of \PubLatMed{}~s after a stop request (maximum
\PubLatMax{}~s). Participants rated all three controllers as safe and could
not tell them apart. The prediction therefore buys safety margin against
measurement error at a small, unnoticed cost in robot availability.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Background}
\IEEEPARstart{I}{nstalling} ceiling panels involves lifting, aligning and fixing
a panel overhead (Fig.~\ref{fig:task}). A robot can hold the panel while the
worker provides judgement and fine hand skills. However, the worker moves around
during the task, the panel enlarges the space the robot occupies, and
unnecessary robot stops can break the flow of work.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=.5\columnwidth]{figures/task-frame.jpg}
\caption{The ceiling-panel installation task in our lab: the robot holds the panel at ceiling height while the worker fixes it from below (frame from session video; face blurred).}
\label{fig:task}
\end{figure}

Speed and separation monitoring (SSM) keeps enough distance between a person
and a robot, and slows or stops the robot in time
\cite{iso10218_2_2025,iso15066,marvel2017implementing}. The simplest form uses
fixed distance thresholds. These are easy to understand, but they treat a
person walking toward the robot, standing still and walking away in the same
way. Knowing the task phase could help the robot avoid unnecessary stops, as
long as a wrongly recognised phase can never loosen the safety limit. Our
controller therefore keeps three parts separate: task-phase recognition,
motion prediction and the distance limit.

The research question is whether a controller that knows the task phase can
keep people as safe as the fixed-zone control used in practice today, while
keeping the task efficient. The planned comparison covers response
to intrusion, unnecessary stops, task efficiency and participant experience.
This exploratory analysis answers three supporting questions: (1) how
accurately the model recognises the task phase in real time; (2) how the
distance measurement and the scripted movements affect how the recorded robot
behaviour should be read; and (3) what the questionnaires show about
participants' experience across the task blocks. Our main hypothesis (H1) is
that the context-aware predictive controller is as safe as fixed-zone control,
the current industry approach, while maintaining comparable task efficiency.
Its safety rests on the same distance limit: the learned parts are not allowed
to relax it.

This work makes three contributions: (1) a physical ceiling-panel prototype in
which task-phase recognition and intrusion prediction are kept separate, and
both sit below a fixed speed limit; (2) an exact same-input comparison of the
three controllers on the recorded movement of all 12 participants, including
measured robot stopping and sensitivity to distance error; and (3) participant
ratings linked to the controller that ran in each block. The
contribution is combining and testing these parts in a construction task, not
a new SSM formula.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Human--Robot Collaboration in Construction}
Construction robotics is moving from fully automated machines toward systems
that combine human skill with robot strength
\cite{bock2015construction,zhang2023construction}. Reviews of human--robot
collaboration (HRC) in construction list safety, sensing, communication and
task sharing as the main barriers to use on site
\cite{wei2023construction,lin2025safety}. Panel installation is a useful test
case because the person and the robot work around the same object, and
stopping too often directly slows the work.

\subsection{Speed and Separation Monitoring}
SSM needs more than a fixed safety radius around the robot. The required
distance depends on how far the person and the robot move while the system
senses, decides and brakes. It also includes margins for body parts that are
not tracked and for sensor error \cite{marvel2017implementing,iso15066}.
Byner et al.\ \cite{byner2019dynamic} set the robot's speed limit from the
separation distance and direction of motion, and measured cycle performance in
a physical machine-tending task. Karagiannis et al.\
\cite{karagiannis2022adaptive} switch between safety-zone layouts depending on
where the robot is, using an overhead safety camera, a safety-rated
programmable logic controller (PLC) and the robot controller in a cell modelled
on the car industry. These studies show that adaptive SSM and its effect on
productivity have already been studied. They also define the reactive
controller that task context must improve on.

Construction studies narrow the gap further. Xiao Lin et al.\
\cite{lin2025digitaltwin} combine worker, robot and material shapes in a digital
twin that recalculates the protective distance as the scene changes, and test
it in static and moving conflict scenarios. Their treatment of carried material
matters for panel installation: once the robot carries a panel, a shape drawn
around the robot alone no longer covers the space it occupies.

\subsection{Activity Recognition and Collaboration}
A hidden Markov model (HMM) tracks a state that changes over time but cannot be
observed directly. It uses a transition matrix, which gives how likely the
state is to change, and an emission model, which gives the measurements each
state tends to produce \cite{rabiner1989}. HMMs have been used to recognise
activities from wearable sensors and to model human--robot tasks
\cite{manouchehri2023hmm,berg2018assembly}.

Task phase and hazard are different things. Labels such as ``working'' or
``retreating'' describe what the person is doing, while a hazard is about
being exposed to harm. A person can trip, or move quickly toward the robot,
during any phase. Treating ``hazard'' as just another phase would mix up
activity and danger. We therefore use the HMM only to estimate the task phase.
Fast movement toward the robot is detected separately by the intrusion
predictor (Section~\ref{sec:context}), which uses only the measured distance
and closing speed. Keeping the two apart also means the distance limit can be
tested on its own, whatever the HMM decides.

The closest construction study, by Xiao Lin et al.\ \cite{lin2026semantic},
combines a model of the work steps, robot behaviour learned from demonstration
and local safety control in a virtual-reality wall-panel task with simulated
sensors. Our study adds a physical ceiling-panel prototype with real robot
data and participant questionnaires.

How smoothly people and robots work together is usually measured with timing,
such as human idle time, robot idle time, time spent working in parallel and
delay between actions \cite{hoffman2019fluency}. Workload and trust
questionnaires help explain results, but they cannot prove physical safety or
replace measured robot response \cite{hart1988tlx,jian2000trust}. This paper
therefore links task recognition, measured control inputs and participant
experience in one physical panel task. Table~\ref{tab:prior-work} compares
these approaches with our prototype.

\subsection{Overview}
The controller (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}) has two inputs: the worker's
position and speed relative to the robot, from motion capture, and the position
of the robot's tool, from the robot controller. Its output is a single number
between 0 and 1: the fraction of full speed the robot may use. This number is
sent to the robot's speed override, and a value of zero requests a pause.

\subsection{Equipment and Sensing}
\label{sec:apparatus}
The lab prototype (Fig.~\ref{fig:apparatus}) uses a Universal Robots UR10 arm
with a vacuum gripper that holds a lightweight stand-in for a ceiling panel. The
arm lifts the panel to ceiling height, the participant walks in to work on it,
and the arm then lowers it. Two separate systems track the participant.

\emph{Xsens MVN} is a motion-capture suit. Seventeen small motion sensors, each
with an accelerometer, gyroscope and magnetometer, are strapped to the body.
Software combines their signals with a body model to give the position and
orientation of 23 body segments, such as the pelvis, head, arms and legs, at up
to 240~Hz \cite{roetenberg2009xsens,schepers2018xsens}. Because position is
calculated by adding up small movements over time, it slowly drifts, and its
starting point is set by a calibration pose rather than by the room. Here,
Xsens provides the body-segment positions used for the body-distance input in
the later version of the system.

\emph{OptiTrack} is a camera-based motion-capture system. Infrared cameras
around the cell (on the overhead frame in Fig.~\ref{fig:apparatus}a) detect
small reflective markers, and the Motive software locates each marker to about
a millimetre in a fixed room frame \cite{optitrack2024motive}. A group of
markers on the participant's cap is tracked as one object. OptiTrack therefore
gives the head's position relative to the robot without drift, but says
nothing about the rest of the body. Here, the OptiTrack head position is used
to calculate the distance and closing speed that drive the phase model and the
reactive limit.

The two systems have different jobs, and their estimates are not merged:
OptiTrack gives the head's position relative to the robot, and Xsens gives body
posture. Both feed the same processing step in the same format, so the
controller does not depend on which system produced a sample. The robot
controller reports the tool centre point (TCP), which is the position of the
gripper face, and the speed of each of the six joints. The control loop runs
at about 60~Hz, and the actual time between samples is logged.

\subsection{Distance Measures and System Versions}
The robot is represented by a vertical column, a simple stand-in for its shape.
For the tracked head position $\mathbf p$ and the nearest point $\mathbf q$ on
this column, the controller calculates
\begin{equation}
d=\|\mathbf q-\mathbf p\|_2,\qquad
v_{\rm proj}=\mathbf v^{\mathsf T}\mathbf u,
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the head-to-robot distance in metres, $\mathbf v$ is the head
velocity estimated from recent samples, $\mathbf u$ is the unit vector pointing
from the head toward the robot, and $v_{\rm proj}$ is the closing speed in m/s.
A positive $v_{\rm proj}$ means the person is moving toward the robot. The
heading alignment $h_t$, used below, measures how directly the person's
movement points at the robot; it is not the direction the body faces.
Table~\ref{tab:notation} lists all symbols.

Data were recorded with two versions of the system. (In this paper a code such

The phase model used head-based measures in both versions. The two versions are

\subsection{Task Phase and Intrusion Prediction}
\label{sec:context}
The model recognises three task phases: approaching, working and retreating. At
each time step it uses four measures,
$\mathbf x_t=[d_t,v_{{\rm proj},t},\|\mathbf v_t\|,h_t]^{\mathsf T}$: distance,
closing speed, overall speed and heading alignment. For each phase, the
typical values of these measures are described by a mix of three Gaussian
(bell-shaped) distributions:
\begin{equation}
p(\mathbf x_t\mid z_t=i)=\sum_{k=1}^{3}w_{ik}
\mathcal N(\mathbf x_t;\boldsymbol\mu_{ik},\operatorname{diag}
(\boldsymbol\sigma^2_{ik})),
\end{equation}
where $z_t$ is the phase at time $t$, $w_{ik}$ is the weight of component $k$
in phase $i$ (the three weights add to one), and $\boldsymbol\mu_{ik}$ and
$\boldsymbol\sigma^2_{ik}$ are that component's mean and variance for each
measure. In real time the model can use only present and past data (the
causal filter). For offline checking, the Viterbi method can also use later
data, so results for the two are reported separately. Because the labelled
phases are long and the model expects phases to persist, it changes phase
slowly and may react late to short movements.

The intrusion predictor works independently of the HMM. It solves
\begin{equation}
d-r_{\rm red}-v_{\rm proj}\tau-\tfrac12 a_{\rm proj}\tau^2=0
\end{equation}
for $\tau$, the time the person would take to reach the inner (red) boundary of
radius $r_{\rm red}$ from the current distance $d$ if they kept their current
closing speed $v_{\rm proj}$ and acceleration $a_{\rm proj}$. If $\tau$ is
between zero and 0.5~s, the controller flags an intrusion. The closing-speed
threshold is 0.6~m/s, and the acceleration used is capped at
$\pm4$~m/s$^2$. The predictor only detects movement toward the boundary; it
does not guess the person's intention.

\subsection{Controllers and Speed Command}
Three controllers were compared. The \emph{fixed} controller uses set distance
bands: full speed, reduced speed and stop. The \emph{reactive} controller also
takes closing speed into account, using a simplified separation limit. The
\emph{predictive} controller adds phase-based caution and the intrusion
predictor on top of the reactive limit. Its protective separation distance is
\begin{equation}
S=\max(0,v_{\rm proj})T+C+S_a,
\end{equation}
where $S$ is the distance the controller tries to keep; $T$ is the assumed time
for the system to react and the robot to stop, so $v_{\rm proj}T$ is how much
closer the person gets in that time; $C$ is a margin for body parts that the
tracked point does not cover; and $S_a$ is a margin for sensor error. This is
the ISO/TS 15066 separation formula without the term for the robot's own
movement \cite{iso15066}. We used $T=0.4$~s, $C=0.2$~m and $S_a=0.1$~m. Beyond
$S$, the allowed speed rises gradually over 0.3~m. The inner (red) radius is
0.94~m, and the yellow radius is 1.6 times that. These values were chosen for
the lab cell; they are not measured stopping times or sensor errors of an
installed system.

The speed sent to the robot is
\begin{equation}
u_{\rm cmd}=\min(u_{\rm env},u_{\rm context}),\qquad 0\le u_{\rm cmd}\le1,
\label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
where $u_{\rm env}$ is the speed fraction allowed by the reactive limit,
$u_{\rm context}$ is the fraction suggested by the phase model and intrusion
predictor, and $u_{\rm cmd}$ is the fraction sent to the robot. Taking the
smaller of the two means the learned parts can only lower the speed, never
raise it. Inside the red radius the robot is always asked to stop, and if
tracking is lost the controller falls back to its most cautious setting. The
command is sent through the robot's speed override and pause function. This
is a research controller: its stop is not a safety-rated protective stop, and
(\ref{eq:bound}) limits the software request rather than certifying the
installed system (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\aidraft{Two details of the controller matter for the results. First, the HMM's
phase caution can only cap the speed at the reduced level, so it never stops
the robot on its own. Second, the intrusion predictor issues a stop once the
risk has stayed above its threshold for two consecutive samples (about 33~ms
at 60~Hz) and releases it as soon as the risk falls back below the same
threshold; there is no separate, lower release threshold.}

\section{Model Development and Validation}
We trained the phase model before the participant study, using 32 labelled
trials in which the three team members acted as operators (study IDs P03,
eight trials; P04, 12; P05, 12). These contain 90,487 frames: 17,267
approaching, 53,609 working and 19,611 retreating. We validated it by leaving
one operator out at a time: the model was trained on two operators and tested
on the third.
\input{tables/model_validation}
Two measures are reported. Accuracy is the share of frames whose predicted
phase matches the label. Because most frames are ``working'', a model that
favours that phase scores well on accuracy. Balanced accuracy averages the
recall of the three phases, so each phase counts equally. Offline accuracy is
0.821 and real-time (causal) accuracy is 0.794 (Table~\ref{tab:model-validation}).
Balanced accuracy is lower, especially for the causal filter (0.725), and recall
is highest for the working phase. The small number of operators, the
correlation between frames in the same trial, and the reuse of the same folds
during development limit how far these values generalise.
The \PubPeople{} participants were never used in training, so they form a further
held-out test. Against the planned-cue phase labels, the real-time model was
correct a median of \PubPhaseAccMed\% of the time per person (range
\PubPhaseAccLo--\PubPhaseAccHi\%; balanced accuracy \PubPhaseBalMed\%). The labels
mark when a cue was given rather than when the movement began, so this is a
lower bound. Phase errors change only the yellow-zone speed limit, not stops.

\subsection{Task and Experimental Design}
The protocol has three blocks, A, B and C. The fixed, reactive and predictive
controllers were assigned to the blocks in one of six rotated orders. Each
block contains one clean trial, one distractor trial and one rapid-movement
trial (Fig.~\ref{fig:procedure}). Each trial follows the panel sequence: walk
in and load the panel, step back before the lift, work under the raised panel,
and step back before it is lowered and unloaded. The operator gave the scripted
cue during the lift, so a cue window marks when the instruction was given, not
when the movement started.

\subsection{Participants and Analysed Records}
\label{sec:participants}
The study ran under Monash University ethics approval (project 51642). Twelve
people took part. Some sessions were restarted under a new P-number, so we
checked identity from the data before analysis. Each analysed study code is one
continuous sitting of 25 to 35 minutes that contains a full rotated sequence of
the three controllers, separated from the next sitting by about 30 minutes.
The Xsens body profile was sometimes reused between people (two pairs of codes
have identical segment lengths), so it cannot identify a person; the height of
the tracked helmet, measured independently by OptiTrack, separates those codes
(for example 1.75 against 1.81~m, with a spread of about $\pm$0.01~m within a
code). One person was recorded under two codes on 17 September, confirmed by
identical intake answers; these are merged.

Eleven people have eight or nine analysed trials each (97 trials; two planned
slots had no completed trial). The twelfth person has eight trials (three
reactive and five predictive, with no fixed-zone block) that were excluded from
the earlier analysis for phase order and a lower capture rate (about 26~Hz);
they are included here because the same-input replay
(Section~\ref{sec:telemetry}) gives all three controllers on their recorded
movement. In total there are \PubTrials{} trials. Eleven people gave 31 block
questionnaires (two are missing the last block); the twelfth completed only the
intake form. Pilot, rehearsal and team sessions are excluded. No missing values
were filled in.

\subsection{Robot Data Processing}
\label{sec:telemetry}
The selection above gave 97 trials and 494,075 samples. File fingerprints,
row counts and time totals were rechecked independently after the analysis.
For time totals, each sample's value is held until the next sample, but only
when the gap to the next sample is between 0 and 0.25~s. Longer gaps are
reported separately and left out of both the total and the time base. A
robot-data sample counts as fresh when the packet is available, all six joint
speeds are valid and the data are less than 0.25~s old. The robot counts as
moving when its fastest joint exceeds 0.005~rad/s; counts at 0.001 and
0.01~rad/s are kept as a check on this threshold.

To check the head distance, we recalculated it from the same sample's head
position and the robot's live tool position. This checks internal
consistency, not physical calibration. For each cue window we report the
smallest distance and the largest closing speed, which need not occur at the
same moment. Stop time includes intentional holds in the task, so it is not a
count of false stops.

\textbf{Same-input replay.} The controllers are fixed rules applied to logged
inputs (distance, closing speed and acceleration, and body features), so each
can be re-run on any recorded trial. We replayed all three controllers on the
recorded inputs of every trial, with the same fail-safe holds the live system
applied to all of them. Replay used the exact code and model version that ran:
the first participant's sitting used an earlier version of the predictive
controller's yellow-zone rule, and this was reproduced. The replayed command
matched the logged robot command in every sample of all \PubTrials{} trials,
and, in the later trials where the system also logged the other two
controllers' requests, it matched those too. This gives a paired comparison of
the three controllers on identical human movement, free of differences in
person, order, learning and fatigue. It is open loop: it shows what each
controller would have requested, not how the person or robot would then have
moved.

\textbf{Measures.} A stop is \emph{unnecessary} if the person is outside the
red boundary and does not enter it within the next $H$~s ($H=1$~s; 0.5 and 2~s
give the same ranking). For each entry into the red boundary, \emph{lead} is
how long a stop had already been requested. To test sensitivity to distance
error, entries and coverage are recomputed with the red boundary moved by
$\pm$5 and $\pm$10~cm while the controllers are unchanged. Robot stopping time
is measured from a stop request made while the tool was moving to the first
sample at which the measured tool speed is below 0.005~m/s. Stop requests are
also classed as brief (under 0.5~s) or sustained.

\textbf{Statistics.} The person is the unit of analysis. Quantities are
totalled per person and divided by that person's observed time. Paired
differences are tested with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, with 95\%
confidence intervals from a bootstrap over people.

\subsection{Questionnaire Inclusion and Scoring}
Pilot, link-test and qualification submissions were excluded. This left
\IntakeCodes{} intake forms, \BlockResponses{} block forms and
\FollowupCodes{} end forms, with 11, 11 and 9 block forms for A, B and C and no
duplicates.

The block questionnaire uses five-point ratings. Three items run between two
opposite words: anxious to relaxed, agitated to calm, and quiescent to
surprised. Four items ask how much the participant agrees that they were
confident the robot would stop, had to watch it constantly, felt it stopped
more than needed, and found its speed changes made sense. Three items rate
mental demand, physical demand and frustration. The workload items are adapted
from NASA-TLX \cite{hart1988tlx} and the confidence and monitoring items from
Jian et al.\ \cite{jian2000trust}. Neither set is the full validated
questionnaire, so each item is analysed on its own and no combined score is
formed.

Every trial record states its block and the controller that ran, so each
block questionnaire is linked to a controller. Items are compared between
controllers with the Friedman test (people with all three blocks) and paired
Wilcoxon tests, and against block position (first, second, third) to separate
controller from order. End-form choices are mapped to controllers and compared
with chance. Whether felt unnecessary stopping follows measured unnecessary
stopping is tested with a within-person (repeated-measures) correlation. The
intake and end forms ask about expected and then experienced safety and trust,
from 1 (not at all) to 5 (completely).

\subsection{Robot Data}
The 97 trials of the eleven people with full sessions are summarised by the
controller that actually ran (Table~\ref{tab:controllers}); these logged totals
mix person, order and system version, and Section~\ref{sec:same-input} gives
the paired comparison. The 97
trials contain 494,075 samples and 186.17 minutes of capture. 398 gaps are
longer than 250~ms, totalling 281.65~s (2.52\%); 10,888.35~s remain for time
totals. 3244 samples are flagged as stale tracking, and 395 put the controller
into its most cautious state. Controller decisions exist in 494,023 samples,
and every one is recorded as sent to the robot. This confirms that commands
were recorded, not how the robot physically stopped.

The trials are close to the planned 11 per trial type per controller; the two
missing slots leave 10 clean fixed and 10 clean reactive trials. Median stop
time is 29.7\%, 33.6\% and 36.7\% for fixed, reactive and predictive records,
and the raw numbers of stop requests are 659, 888 and 1417. Stop time also
includes intentional task holds.

The safety indicators were similar across controllers
(Table~\ref{tab:controllers}). Whenever the measured distance was inside the
0.94-m red boundary, all three controllers requested a stop 100\% of the time.
The median closest distance per trial was 0.08, 0.06 and 0.08~m, and people
spent 28.5\%, 31.3\% and 32.0\% of observed time inside the red boundary,
mostly during the loading, working and unloading approaches the task requires.
Median trial duration was 112.5, 113.6 and 109.0~s, so predictive trials were
not slower overall, although the robot spent more of that time stopped.

\textbf{Distance error.} The 100\% coverage holds at the measured distance
because all controllers share the red boundary. The reused body profiles put
modelled body size off by up to about 5~cm. If the true distance were 5~cm
smaller than measured, a stop would already have been requested at

\PubPeople{} people, and the result is the same at 10~cm. Early stopping
therefore turns a small lead time into a large margin against measurement

\subsection{Distance Inputs and Cued Movements}
In the early records, the median difference between the recorded head distance
and the same-sample recalculation (95th percentile per trial) is 0.481~m
(Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry}a). In the later records it is 0.000~m at the reported
precision. This shows that the later logging is arithmetically consistent, not
that it is physically calibrated or safety-rated.

The wide spread and overlap of rapid and distractor cue windows

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{What Prediction Buys}
The prototype combines the task phase and motion prediction while keeping a
fixed software limit on the commanded speed, so a wrong phase can never allow a
higher speed than the distance limit. For the same inputs the predictive
controller can therefore never request a higher speed than the reactive one;
any benefit must come from when it intervenes. The same-input replay measures
exactly this. Prediction moved the stop request earlier for every participant,
and under a few centimetres of distance error that earlier request covered
roughly twice as many boundary entries as fixed-zone control. The value of
prediction in this task is therefore robustness to measurement error, which
matters because body distance is the least certain input in the system.

The cost is brief switching. The intrusion predictor stops the robot after two
samples above its threshold and releases as soon as the risk drops, so a noisy
closing-speed estimate near the threshold makes the command flip between stop
and run. This added under one second of unnecessary stopping per minute, and
participants did not notice it: no rating differed between controllers. A
release delay or hysteresis on the predictor should remove most of the
switching without touching the fixed distance limit, but a simple minimum stop
time is not a free fix, because it lengthens every stop.

\aidraft{Against H1, the predictive controller was at least as safe as
fixed-zone control on every measure we could compute: it had requested a stop
at every boundary entry, earlier than the other controllers, and with more
margin against distance error. Comparable efficiency was not fully shown: trials
took about the same time, but the robot was stopped for slightly more of each
trial. H1 is therefore supported on safety and partly supported on efficiency.}

\subsection{The Task Limits the Gain}
Under all three controllers the robot was stopped for about a third of each
trial, and almost all of that stopping was followed by the worker entering the
red boundary within a second. The panel task places the worker inside the
protective distance while loading, working under the panel and unloading, and
several participants pushed the held panel. The productivity limit therefore
comes from using SSM during close-contact phases, not from the choice of SSM
controller. Switching to a contact-capable mode (hand guiding or power and force
limiting) by task phase, which the system design already supports, is the
larger opportunity.

\subsection{Safety and Efficiency}
\label{sec:safety-eff}
Research on adaptive SSM motivates measuring robot availability and cycle
performance \cite{byner2019dynamic,karagiannis2022adaptive}. The measurable part
of the reaction chain fits the assumed $T=0.4$~s: Xsens data were at most
\PubXsensAgePninefive{}~s old in 95\% of control steps, a step took at most
\PubTickPninefive{}~s, and the robot stopped within \PubLatMax{}~s, giving
about 0.31~s at the 95th percentile. The rarest cases reach about 0.44~s, and
the processing delay inside the motion-capture devices is not visible in the
logs, so a larger $T$ (for example 0.5~s) is advisable. Stop time combines
intentional holds and responses to people nearby, and capture time includes
operator actions, so neither is a direct measure of task efficiency or
collaboration fluency \cite{hoffman2019fluency}. The command used the ordinary
robot speed override rather than a safety-rated stop, so none of these results
support a certified safety claim.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
The sample is small (12 people) and was recruited for convenience; one
participant lacks the fixed-zone block and the questionnaires after the intake.
The system version changed during collection: the earlier six participants
were measured with a head-only distance, the later six with a whole-body
distance, and the first participant used an earlier version of the predictive
yellow-zone rule. The Xsens body profile was reused between some people, which
introduces up to about 5~cm of distance error; Section~\ref{sec:same-input}
reports how much this matters. The same-input comparison is open loop, so it
does not show how people would have moved under a different controller. The
unnecessary-stop measure uses hindsight over a 1-s window and the red boundary
as the reference, and the phase labels mark cues rather than observed movement.
Cued movements, operator pacing, learning and fatigue may affect behaviour and
ratings; the rotated block order controls for order but not for these.

\subsection{Future Work}
A confirmatory session should run the predictive controller with a release
delay on the intrusion predictor, a reaction time of at least 0.5~s, freshly
measured body profiles for each person, one frozen system version and an ID that
follows each person. It should also switch to a contact-capable mode while the
worker is under the panel, since that is where most of the stopping occurs.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presented a speed and separation monitoring controller that uses the
task phase and motion prediction in human--robot ceiling-panel installation,
and tested it on a physical prototype with \PubPeople{} participants. Replaying
all three controllers exactly on the identical recorded movement showed that
every controller had requested a stop at all \PubEntries{} entries into the red
boundary. The predictive controller requested stops \PubLeadPF{}~s earlier than
fixed-zone control for every participant, which under a 5-cm distance error
raised the share of entries already covered from \PubCovFixedFive\% to
\PubCovPredFive\%. Its cost was \PubUnnecPF{}~s per minute of extra unnecessary
stopping, caused by brief switching of the intrusion predictor, which
participants did not notice and which a release delay should remove. The robot
stopped within \PubLatMax{}~s of a request. The largest remaining gain lies not in
the choice of SSM controller but in switching to a contact-capable mode while
the worker is under the panel.
