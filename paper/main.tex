\begin{abstract}
Ceiling-panel installation is demanding overhead work that a collaborative
robot could assist. In the human--robot version studied here, a worker and a
robot share a workspace while handling the same panel. Fixed safety zones cannot tell whether the worker is
approaching, working or moving away, so they can stop the robot more often than
needed. This paper presents a speed and separation monitoring (SSM) controller
that uses the task phase, and tests it on a physical prototype. A hidden Markov
model (HMM) estimates the task phase from motion-capture data, and a separate
predictor detects fast movement toward the robot. A fixed rule ensures that
neither of these learned parts can make the robot faster than the speed limit
set by the distance-based reactive controller. Tested on operators it had not been trained on,
the real-time phase model was correct 79.4\% of the time (balanced accuracy
72.5\%). The final analysed dataset contained 97 robot-data trials from 11 study codes
under fixed, reactive and predictive control, plus 31 block questionnaires.
Participants were confident that the robot would stop and reported low
workload, and their average safety rating rose from 3.82/5 before the session
to 4.45/5 after it. We hypothesised that the predictive controller is as
safe as fixed-zone control, the current industry approach, while keeping
comparable efficiency. The safety indicators matched: every controller
requested a stop whenever a person was inside the red boundary, and closest
approach and time inside it were similar. Trials took about as long, but the
predictive controller kept the robot stopped for more of each trial, so the
hypothesis is only partly supported. The study also sets out what must be measured to compare the controllers
conclusively.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Background}
\IEEEPARstart{I}{nstalling} ceiling panels involves lifting, aligning and fixing
panels overhead, work that is physically demanding and usually done by people
alone or with lifting aids. Collaborative robots could take over the lifting
and holding \cite{zhang2023construction}; the arrangement adopted in this study
is for a robot to hold the panel while the worker provides judgement and fine hand
skills (Fig.~\ref{fig:scenario}a). The worker then moves around a robot that is
carrying a panel, the panel enlarges the space the robot occupies, and
unnecessary robot stops can break the flow of work.

Speed and separation monitoring (SSM) keeps enough distance between a person
and a robot, and slows or stops the robot in time
\cite{iso10218_2_2025,iso15066,marvel2017implementing}. The simplest form uses
fixed distance thresholds. These are easy to understand, but they treat a
person walking toward the robot, standing still and walking away in the same
way. Knowing the task phase could help the robot avoid unnecessary stops, as
long as a wrongly recognised phase can never loosen the safety limit. Our
controller therefore keeps three parts separate: task-phase recognition,
motion prediction and the distance limit.

The research question is whether a controller that knows the task phase can
keep people as safe as the fixed-zone control used in practice today, while
keeping the task efficient. The planned comparison covers response
to intrusion, unnecessary stops, task efficiency and participant experience.
This exploratory analysis answers three supporting questions: (1) how
accurately the model recognises the task phase in real time; (2) how the
distance measurement and the scripted movements affect how the recorded robot
behaviour should be read; and (3) what the questionnaires show about
participants' experience across the task blocks. Our main hypothesis (H1) is
that the context-aware predictive controller is as safe as fixed-zone control,
the current industry approach, while maintaining comparable task efficiency.
Its safety rests on the same distance limit: the learned parts are not allowed
to relax it.

This work makes three contributions: (1) a physical ceiling-panel prototype in
which task-phase recognition and intrusion prediction are kept separate, and
both sit below a fixed speed limit; (2) a descriptive analysis of the distance
inputs and robot motion recorded during the task; and (3) item-by-item
questionnaire results, checked again using only complete response sets. The
contribution is combining and testing these parts in a construction task, not
a new SSM formula.

\subsection{Human--Robot Collaboration in Construction}
Construction robotics is moving from fully automated machines toward systems
that combine human skill with robot strength
\cite{bock2015construction,zhang2023construction}. Reviews of human--robot
collaboration (HRC) in construction list safety, sensing, communication and
task sharing as the main barriers to use on site
\cite{wei2023construction,lin2025safety}. Panel installation is a useful test
case because the person and the robot work around the same object, and
stopping too often directly slows the work.

\subsection{Speed and Separation Monitoring}
SSM needs more than a fixed safety radius around the robot. The required
distance depends on how far the person and the robot move while the system
senses, decides and brakes. It also includes margins for body parts that are
not tracked and for sensor error \cite{marvel2017implementing,iso15066}.
Byner et al.\ \cite{byner2019dynamic} set the robot's speed limit from the
separation distance and direction of motion, and measured cycle performance in
a physical machine-tending task. Karagiannis et al.\
\cite{karagiannis2022adaptive} switch between safety-zone layouts depending on
where the robot is, using an overhead safety camera, a safety-rated
programmable logic controller (PLC) and the robot controller in a cell modelled
on the car industry. Adaptive SSM and its effect on productivity have therefore already been
studied, and they define the reactive baseline that task context must improve
on.

Construction studies narrow the gap further. Xiao Lin et al.\
\cite{lin2025digitaltwin} combine worker, robot and material shapes in a digital
twin that recalculates the protective distance as the scene changes, and test
it in static and moving conflict scenarios. Their treatment of carried material
matters for panel installation: once the robot carries a panel, a shape drawn
around the robot alone no longer covers the space it occupies.

\subsection{Activity Recognition and Collaboration}
A hidden Markov model (HMM) tracks a state that changes over time but cannot be
observed directly. It uses a transition matrix, which gives how likely the
state is to change, and an emission model, which gives the measurements each
state tends to produce \cite{rabiner1989}. HMMs have been used to recognise
activities from wearable sensors and to model human--robot tasks
\cite{manouchehri2023hmm,berg2018assembly}.

In these studies the recognised states describe activities; whether hazards
belong among them is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:context}.

The closest construction study, by Xiao Lin et al.\ \cite{lin2026semantic},
combines a model of the work steps, robot behaviour learned from demonstration
and local safety control in a virtual-reality wall-panel task with simulated
sensors. Our study adds a physical ceiling-panel prototype with real robot
data and participant questionnaires.

How smoothly people and robots work together is usually measured with timing,
such as human idle time, robot idle time, time spent working in parallel and
delay between actions \cite{hoffman2019fluency}. Workload and trust
questionnaires help explain results, but they cannot prove physical safety or
replace measured robot response \cite{hart1988tlx,jian2000trust}. This paper
therefore links task recognition, measured control inputs and participant
experience in one physical panel task. Table~\ref{tab:prior-work} compares
these approaches with our prototype.

\subsection{Overview}
The controller (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}) has two inputs: from motion
capture, the position of the worker's head relative to the robot and the
head's velocity in the room frame; and from the robot controller, the position
of the robot's tool. Its output is a single number
between 0 and 1: the fraction of full speed the robot may use. This number is
sent to the robot's speed override, and a value of zero requests a pause.

\subsection{Equipment and Sensing}
\label{sec:apparatus}
The lab prototype (Fig.~\ref{fig:apparatus}) uses a Universal Robots UR10 arm
with a vacuum gripper that holds a lightweight stand-in for a ceiling panel. The
arm lifts the panel to ceiling height, the participant walks in to work on it,
and the arm then lowers it. Two separate systems track the participant.

\emph{Xsens MVN} is a motion-capture suit. Seventeen small motion sensors, each
with an accelerometer, gyroscope and magnetometer, are strapped to the body.
Software combines their signals with a body model to give the position and
orientation of 23 body segments, such as the pelvis, head, arms and legs, at up
to 240~Hz \cite{roetenberg2009xsens,schepers2018xsens}. Because position is
calculated by adding up small movements over time, it slowly drifts, and its
starting point is set by a calibration pose rather than by the room. Here,
Xsens provides the body-segment positions used for the body-distance input in
the later version of the system.

\emph{OptiTrack} is a camera-based motion-capture system. Infrared cameras
around the cell (on the overhead frame in Fig.~\ref{fig:apparatus}a) detect
small reflective markers, and the Motive software locates each marker to about
a millimetre in a fixed room frame \cite{optitrack2024motive}. A group of
markers on the participant's cap is tracked as one object. OptiTrack therefore
gives the head's position relative to the robot without drift, but says
nothing about the rest of the body. Here, the OptiTrack head position is used
to calculate the distance and closing speed that drive the phase model and the
reactive limit.

The two systems' estimates are not merged; both feed the same processing step
in the same format. The robot
controller reports the tool centre point (TCP), which is the position of the
gripper face, and the speed of each of the six joints. The control loop runs
at about 60~Hz, and the actual time between samples is logged.

\subsection{Distance Measures and System Versions}
The robot is represented by a vertical column, a simple stand-in for its shape
(Fig.~\ref{fig:scenario}b). The column is a vertical line segment, with no
radius, through the horizontal position of a robot reference point; it runs
from the robot-base plane ($z=0$) up to the height of that point. All positions
are in the robot-base frame. For the tracked head position $\mathbf p$, the
nearest point $\mathbf q$ on the column lies on the column at the head's
height, limited to the column's height range. The controller calculates
\begin{equation}
d=\|\mathbf q-\mathbf p\|_2,\qquad
v_{\rm proj}=\mathbf v^{\mathsf T}\mathbf u,
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the head-to-robot distance in metres, $\mathbf v$ is the head
velocity in the room frame (the least-squares slope of the last five samples;
the robot's own motion is not subtracted), $\mathbf u$ is the unit vector pointing
from the head toward the robot, and $v_{\rm proj}$ is the closing speed in m/s.
A positive $v_{\rm proj}$ means the person is moving toward the robot. The
heading alignment $h_t$, used below, measures how directly the person's
movement points at the robot; it is not the direction the body faces.
Table~\ref{tab:notation} lists all symbols.

Data were recorded with two system versions that place the column
differently. In the earlier version the reference point was fixed on the
robot base's vertical axis, 2.2~m high, so the column did not follow the arm.
In the later version it is the live TCP, read at every step and used only if
less than 0.25~s old, so the column moves with the gripper and panel. The later
version also streamed data automatically rather than on operator
confirmation, and for control it used the 23 Xsens body segments, placed at the
OptiTrack cap and taking the smallest distance and largest closing speed. The
phase model used head measures in both. The versions are reported separately
(Section~\ref{sec:participants}), not as an experimental condition.

\subsection{Task Phase and Intrusion Prediction}
\label{sec:context}
The model recognises three task phases: approaching, working and retreating.
These labels describe what the person is doing, whereas a hazard is about being
exposed to harm, and a person can trip or move quickly toward the robot during
any phase. Treating ``hazard'' as a fourth phase would therefore mix up activity
and danger. The HMM estimates only the task phase, and fast movement toward the
robot is detected separately by the intrusion predictor below, so the distance
limit can be tested on its own whatever the HMM decides. At
each time step it uses four measures,
$\mathbf x_t=[d_t,v_{{\rm proj},t},\|\mathbf v_t\|,h_t]^{\mathsf T}$: distance,
closing speed, overall speed and heading alignment. For each phase, the
typical values of these measures are described by a mix of three Gaussian
(bell-shaped) distributions:
\begin{equation}
p(\mathbf x_t\mid z_t=i)=\sum_{k=1}^{3}w_{ik}
\mathcal N(\mathbf x_t;\boldsymbol\mu_{ik},\operatorname{diag}
(\boldsymbol\sigma^2_{ik})),
\end{equation}
where $z_t$ is the phase at time $t$, $w_{ik}$ is the weight of component $k$
in phase $i$ (the three weights add to one), and $\boldsymbol\mu_{ik}$ and
$\boldsymbol\sigma^2_{ik}$ are that component's mean and variance for each
measure. In real time the model can use only present and past data (the
causal filter). For offline checking, the Viterbi method can also use later
data, so results for the two are reported separately. Because the labelled
phases are long and the model expects phases to persist, it changes phase
slowly and may react late to short movements.

The intrusion predictor works independently of the HMM. It solves
\begin{equation}
d-r_{\rm red}-v_{\rm proj}\tau-\tfrac12 a_{\rm proj}\tau^2=0
\end{equation}
for $\tau$, the time the person would take to reach the inner (red) boundary of
radius $r_{\rm red}$ from the current distance $d$ if they kept their current
closing speed $v_{\rm proj}$ and acceleration $a_{\rm proj}$. An intrusion is
flagged when a risk score that rises as $\tau$ falls below 0.5~s passes its
threshold (equivalent to $\tau\lesssim0.58$~s) while the closing speed is at
least 0.6~m/s. $a_{\rm proj}$ is the slope of the last three closing speeds, a
derivative of a noisy derivative that reached about $\pm30$~m/s$^2$ unclamped
during development, far beyond a walking person. It is therefore capped at
$\pm4$~m/s$^2$ (about 0.4\,$g$), and the 0.6-m/s gate stops sway near the
boundary counting as an approach. Both are engineering settings chosen in
simulation to suppress noise, not measured or standard values. In the later
version the body-segment control input sets $a_{\rm proj}=0$, so the cap had
no effect there. The predictor detects movement toward the boundary, not
intention.

\subsection{Controllers and Speed Command}
Three controllers were compared. The \emph{fixed} controller uses two zones
around the column (Fig.~\ref{fig:scenario}c): inside the red radius it requests
a stop, inside the yellow radius it limits the robot to 35\% of full speed,
and outside both it allows full speed. Leaving a zone requires clearing its
boundary by 0.05~m, so the command does not flicker at the edge. The \emph{reactive} controller also
takes closing speed into account, using a simplified separation limit. The
\emph{predictive} controller adds phase-based caution and the intrusion
predictor on top of the reactive limit. Its protective separation distance is
\begin{equation}
S=\max(0,v_{\rm proj})T+C+S_a,
\end{equation}
where $S$ is the distance the controller tries to keep; $T$ is the assumed time
for the system to react and the robot to stop, so $v_{\rm proj}T$ is how much
closer the person gets in that time; $C$ is a margin for body parts that the
tracked point does not cover; and $S_a$ is a margin for sensor error. This is
the ISO/TS 15066 separation formula without the term for the robot's own
movement \cite{iso15066}. We used $T=0.4$~s, $C=0.2$~m and $S_a=0.1$~m. Beyond
$S$, the allowed speed rises gradually over 0.3~m. The red radius, 0.94~m,
is the same formula evaluated at an assumed approach speed of 1.6~m/s
($1.6T+C+S_a$), and the yellow radius is 1.6 times that (1.50~m). These values were chosen for
the lab cell; they are not measured stopping times or sensor errors of an
installed system.

The speed sent to the robot is
\begin{equation}
u_{\rm cmd}=\min(u_{\rm env},u_{\rm context}),\qquad 0\le u_{\rm cmd}\le1,
\label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
where $u_{\rm env}$ is the speed fraction allowed by the reactive limit,
$u_{\rm context}$ is the fraction suggested by the phase model and intrusion
predictor, and $u_{\rm cmd}$ is the fraction sent to the robot. Taking the
smaller of the two means the learned parts can only lower the speed, never
raise it. Inside the red radius the robot is always asked to stop. If the head
position, body data or robot position are missing or too old (more than
150~ms for OptiTrack or 0.25~s for the robot position), every controller
requests a stop ($u_{\rm cmd}=0$) before its own rules are applied. The
command is sent through the robot's speed override and pause function. This
is a research controller: its stop is not a safety-rated protective stop, and
(\ref{eq:bound}) limits the software request rather than certifying the
installed system (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\aidraft{The HMM's phase caution can only cap the speed at the reduced level, so
it never stops the robot on its own. The intrusion predictor stops once the
risk has exceeded its threshold for two consecutive samples and releases as
soon as it falls back below the same threshold, with no hysteresis.}

\section{Model Development and Validation}
We trained the phase model before the participant study, using 32 labelled
trials in which the three team members acted as operators (study IDs P03,
eight trials; P04, 12; P05, 12). These contain 90,487 frames: 17,267
approaching, 53,609 working and 19,611 retreating. Labels were recorded live:
the experimenter pressed a key at each real phase change to advance the
dashboard's fixed task sequence, and each frame carries the current phase.
\emph{Approaching} runs from the start cue until the participant reaches the
gripper with the panel or stands under the raised panel; \emph{working} runs
until the panel is gripped or, in the second cycle, a brief hazard movement is
cued; \emph{retreating} runs until the robot's lift or lowering is confirmed.
Frames during robot motion and the hazard movement are excluded. All 32 trials
follow the scripted order, but the labels were not reviewed afterwards or
checked by a second labeller. We validated it by leaving
one operator out at a time: the model was trained on two operators and tested
on the third.
\input{tables/model_validation}
Two measures are reported. Accuracy is the share of frames whose predicted
phase matches the label. Because most frames are ``working'', a model that
favours that phase scores well on accuracy. Balanced accuracy averages the
recall of the three phases, so each phase counts equally. Offline accuracy is
0.821 and real-time (causal) accuracy is 0.794 (Table~\ref{tab:model-validation}).
Balanced accuracy is lower, especially for the causal filter (0.725), and recall
is highest for the working phase. Because the controller runs online, the
causal recalls are the relevant ones: 0.684 for approaching, 0.905 for working
and 0.586 for retreating, against 0.693, 0.923 and 0.654 offline. Retreating
loses most, as the filter is slow to leave the working phase. The small number of operators, the
correlation between frames in the same trial, and the reuse of the same folds
during development limit how far these values generalise.

\subsection{Task and Experimental Design}
The protocol has three blocks, A, B and C. The fixed, reactive and predictive
controllers were assigned to the blocks in one of six rotated orders. Each
block contains one clean trial, one distractor trial and one rapid-movement
trial (Fig.~\ref{fig:procedure}). Each trial follows the panel sequence: walk
in and load the panel, step back before the lift, work under the raised panel,
and step back before it is lowered and unloaded. The trial types differ only
during the lift, while the participant waits at the start marker. In a
\emph{clean} trial there is no cue. In a \emph{distractor} trial, on the
operator's ``now'', the participant makes one brisk sideways movement that does
not close on the robot and returns. In a \emph{rapid-movement} trial they make
one brief move toward the robot along a marked direction and return at once
(not a fall, trip or lunge). Both movements were demonstrated and practised
first. A cue window marks the instruction, not the start of the movement.

\subsection{Participants and Analysed Records}
\label{sec:participants}
The study ran under Monash University ethics approval (project 51642). The team
recorded 12 people taking part, but because some sessions were restarted under
a new P-number, that count cannot be rebuilt from the exported records alone.
Study IDs therefore label recorded sessions, not confirmed individuals. In this
paper a code such as P17 is a study ID, and T05 is a trial within it.

Because not every record could be linked to one person, the analysis is
descriptive. The main data set is the 11 study IDs that have both intake and
end forms and a known controller order. These IDs had 99 planned trial slots,
for which 115 attempts were found: 108 completed and seven aborted. For each
slot we used the latest completed trial whose files were readable and whose
sample count matched its record. This gave 97 trials; two slots (P13 block A
trial 1 and P22 block A trial 3) had no completed trial. Earlier repeats of a
slot are kept in the audit record but not analysed. The 52 trials from P13--P22
were recorded with the earlier system version and the 45 from P31--P38 with
the later one (Section~\ref{sec:system}). The same 11 IDs gave 31
block questionnaires: nine IDs completed all three, and two are missing the
last block. All 11 have matched intake and end forms. Robot data and
questionnaires were analysed separately, and all values come directly from
recorded samples and original form responses; no missing values were filled in.

\subsection{Robot Data Processing}
\label{sec:telemetry}
The selection above gave 97 trials and 494,075 samples. File fingerprints,
row counts and time totals were rechecked independently after the analysis.
For time totals, each sample's value is held until the next sample, but only
when the gap to the next sample is between 0 and 0.25~s. Longer gaps are
reported separately and left out of both the total and the time base. A
robot-data sample counts as fresh when the packet is available, all six joint
speeds are valid and the data are less than 0.25~s old. The robot counts as
moving when its fastest joint exceeds 0.005~rad/s; counts at 0.001 and
0.01~rad/s are kept as a check on this threshold.

To check the head distance, we recalculated it from the same sample's head
position and the robot's live tool position. This checks internal
consistency, not physical calibration. For each cue window we report the
smallest distance and the largest closing speed, which need not occur at the
same moment. Stop time includes intentional holds in the task, so it is not a
count of false stops.

\aidraft{The recorded stop counts were checked for a known system issue: the speed
command sometimes switched rapidly between stop and run. To separate this
switching from real stops, every stop request (a change of the command from
above zero to zero) was classed by duration, as \emph{brief} (under 0.5~s) or
\emph{sustained} (0.5~s or longer), and by the controller rule logged with it:
sensing-dropout hold, intrusion predictor, red boundary or envelope minimum.
The same rules were applied to all three controllers, and the raw counts
reproduce the earlier totals for every trial. As a check, stops were also
joined when separated by less than 0.25, 0.5 or 1.0~s and counted only if they
lasted at least 0.5 or 1.0~s. No video was used.}

\subsection{Questionnaire Inclusion and Scoring}
Pilot, link-test and qualification submissions were excluded. This left
\IntakeCodes{} intake forms, \BlockResponses{} block forms and
\FollowupCodes{} end forms, with 11, 11 and 9 block forms for A, B and C and no
duplicates.

The block questionnaire uses five-point ratings. Three items run between two
opposite words: anxious to relaxed, agitated to calm, and quiescent to
surprised. Four items ask how much the participant agrees that they were
confident the robot would stop, had to watch it constantly, felt it stopped
more than needed, and found its speed changes made sense. Three items rate
mental demand, physical demand and frustration. The workload items are adapted
from NASA-TLX \cite{hart1988tlx} and the confidence and monitoring items from
Jian et al.\ \cite{jian2000trust}. Neither set is the full validated
questionnaire, so each item is analysed on its own and no combined score is
formed.

Block results are given as response counts and median [first, third
quartile]. As a check, we repeat the summary using only the nine IDs with all
three block forms, and count how many ratings went up, stayed the same or went
down from A to C. These changes describe the order of the blocks; a higher
rating is not always better. Results are grouped by block label, not by
controller, because the assignment records alone do not prove which controller
each questionnaire refers to. The intake and end forms are matched by study ID
and ask about expected and then experienced safety and trust, from 1 (not at
all) to 5 (completely). Because neither the number of independent people nor
full controller exposure can be confirmed, we report no significance tests,
confidence intervals or effect sizes, and we do not assume missing data are
random.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Robot Data}
Each of the 11 study codes has eight or nine selected trials.
Table~\ref{tab:controllers} summarises them by controller; these totals
describe the records and are not estimates of a controller effect. The 97
trials contain 494,075 samples and 186.17 minutes of capture. 398 gaps are
longer than 250~ms, totalling 281.65~s (2.52\%); 10,888.35~s remain for time
totals. 3244 samples are flagged as stale tracking, and 395 triggered the
tracking-loss stop request. Controller decisions exist in 494,023 samples,
and every one is recorded as sent to the robot. This confirms that commands
were recorded, not how the robot physically stopped.

The trials are close to the planned 11 per trial type per controller; the two
missing slots leave 10 clean fixed and 10 clean reactive trials. Median stop
time is 29.7\%, 33.6\% and 36.7\% for fixed, reactive and predictive records,
and the raw numbers of stop requests are 659, 888 and 1417. Because
controller order, system version and person identity are not jointly
controlled, these differences are descriptive. Stop time also includes
intentional task holds.

The safety indicators were similar across controllers
(Table~\ref{tab:controllers}). Whenever the measured distance was inside the
0.94-m red boundary, all three controllers requested a stop 100\% of the time.
The median closest distance per trial was 0.08, 0.06 and 0.08~m, and people
spent 28.5\%, 31.3\% and 32.0\% of observed time inside the red boundary,
mostly during the loading, working and unloading approaches the task requires.
Median trial duration was 112.5, 113.6 and 109.0~s, so predictive trials were
not slower overall, although the robot spent more of that time stopped.

\subsection{Distance Inputs and Cued Movements}
In the early records, the median difference between the recorded head distance
and the same-sample recalculation (95th percentile per trial) is 0.481~m
(Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry}a). In the later records it is 0.000~m at the reported
precision. The early difference arises because the recorded distance was
measured to the column on the robot-base axis, whereas the recalculation uses
the column under the live TCP, so it grows as the arm carries the panel away
from the base axis. The early distance inputs therefore describe the
controller's view, not the true head-to-panel clearance. The zero difference
shows that the later logging is arithmetically consistent, not
that it is physically calibrated or safety-rated.

The wide spread and overlap of rapid and distractor cue windows
(Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry}b) show that the scripted cue name does not decide the
movement that was actually measured. These values are controller inputs, not
checked physical clearances, so the measured distance and closing speed, not
the cue name, must guide interpretation. Figure~\ref{fig:trace} shows a
typical later record. Because the cue marks the instruction rather than the
start of the movement, the delay between cue and robot response is not
reported as a protective response time.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Context-Aware Control in the Panel Task}
Keeping the task phase and motion prediction below a fixed software limit
separates two questions: whether a movement belongs to a normal task phase, and
whether it is approaching the boundary. A wrong HMM phase cannot raise the
speed above the distance limit, although geometry and timing errors still
affect that limit.

Phase recognition is strongest in the long working phase, and because real-time
balanced accuracy is lower than overall accuracy, phase accuracy alone does
not show practical benefit.
\aidraft{The predictive controller's higher raw stop count is mainly a switching
effect: 805 of its 1417 stop requests were brief requests from the intrusion
predictor, and the HMM caused none. The predictor stops after two samples above
its threshold and releases as soon as the risk drops, so a noisy closing speed
near the threshold flips the command between stop and run; the lower, less even
data rate on later days is consistent with the rise in switching. A release
delay or hysteresis should remove most of this without touching the distance
limit. A minimum stop time is not a free fix: replaying the predictive commands
with a 1-s minimum cut stop requests from 1417 to 420 but raised stopped time
from 1351 to 2064~s.}

Equation~(\ref{eq:bound}) also limits the expected benefit: on the same inputs
the predictive controller can never request a higher speed than the reactive
one, so any benefit must come from the timing of intervention or from how the
person and robot then coordinate, which neither phase accuracy nor replay on
recorded movement can show.

\aidraft{Against H1, the results split into two parts. On safety, we found no
sign that the predictive controller was less safe than fixed-zone control: a
stop was requested every time the measured distance was inside the red
boundary, closest approaches and time inside it were similar, the learned
parts can only lower the speed, and confidence that the robot would stop was
high in every block. On efficiency, trials took about the same time (median
109.0 against 112.5~s), but the robot was stopped for more of each predictive
trial (36.7\% against 29.7\%), with more sustained stops (3.6 against 2.7 per
minute). H1 is therefore partly supported, and because participant, order and
system version were not controlled, it remains inconclusive rather than
confirmed or rejected. Fixing the predictor's switching is the clearest route
to closing the efficiency gap.}

\subsection{Measured Behaviour and Reported Experience}
The difference between the two system versions strongly affects how the
distance input can be read, and the gap between scripted and measured
movements shows that event labels cannot replace measured exposure. Both
support validating the whole chain from sensing to stopping
\cite{marvel2017implementing}.

Participants reported confidence that the robot would stop, low demand and
little frustration, and felt safer after the session than they expected to.
Novelty, supervision, repeated exposure and expectations could all contribute,
so this cannot be credited to the predictive controller. Ceiling and floor
effects leave little room to tell controllers apart, the complete-response
check does not remove bias from missing data, and the preference for block C
does not rank controllers because block labels rotate. Agreement that the
robot stopped more than needed reflects perceived interruption, not an
objective false-stop rate.

\subsection{Safety and Efficiency}
\label{sec:safety-eff}
Research on adaptive SSM motivates measuring robot availability and cycle
performance \cite{byner2019dynamic,karagiannis2022adaptive}. This study builds
what is needed to measure them but does not show an advantage. Stop time
includes intentional holds and capture time includes operator actions, so they
do not establish false stops, task efficiency or fluency
\cite{hoffman2019fluency}. Formally showing equal safety would need a
justified equivalence margin, validated protective geometry and measured
end-to-end stopping response. All planned measures are reported: distance and
stops per controller, trial duration in place of completion time, safety and
trust as intake and end ratings, comfort through the relaxed and calm items,
and predictability through the speed-change item.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
The sample is small and was recruited for convenience. Because sessions were
restarted, not every study ID can be linked to one person, so the 11 study
codes cannot be treated as 11 independent people. Two trial slots lack a
completed capture and two questionnaire sets lack block C, and missing data may
relate to technical problems or task execution. The system version changed
during collection, and the Xsens data rate fell on later days. Head points and
body-segment positions do not cover the full body, tool and panel, and
agreement with a calculation does not prove physical calibration. The
controller is a research prototype: the separation limit is simplified, the
command uses the ordinary robot speed override rather than a safety-rated stop,
and the end-to-end response was not measured, so the results do not support a
certified safety claim. Cued movements, operator pacing, visible robot motion,
learning and fatigue may affect both behaviour and ratings. The phase model was
built from three operators and may not generalise to construction workers.

\subsection{Future Work}
Future tests should keep one system version, check its sensor error over the
full moving volume, add hysteresis to the intrusion predictor, synchronise body
movement, controller output and robot braking to measure response times, and
use a study ID that follows each person across restarted sessions.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presented a speed and separation monitoring controller that uses
the task phase in human--robot ceiling-panel installation, and tested it on a
physical prototype. Phase recognition and fast-approach prediction sit below a
fixed speed limit, so the learned parts can only make the robot more cautious.
In real time, the phase model was correct 79.4\% of the time on unseen
operators. Across 97 robot-data trials and 31 block questionnaires,
participants were confident that the robot would stop, reported low workload,
and felt safer by the end of the session. H1 was partly supported: every
controller requested a stop whenever a person was inside the red boundary,
closest approaches were similar and trials took about as long, but the robot
spent more time stopped under predictive control, mostly because of brief
switching in the intrusion predictor, which makes hysteresis the next design
change. Formally showing equal safety and comparable availability will need a
consistent distance measurement, synchronised measurement of the robot's
response, and an ID that follows each participant across restarted sessions.
