### The system and its design (Zenan)

**What does "task phase" mean, and how does the robot know it?**
The panel task has three phases: approaching (walking in to the gripper or under the raised panel), working (loading the panel or working under it) and retreating (stepping back while the robot lifts or lowers it). A hidden Markov model recognises the phase live from the worker's tracked movement. We trained it on 32 trials of our own team doing the task, labelled live.

**What does your controller do that fixed zones don't?**
Fixed zones look only at distance: stop inside 0.94 m and slow to 35% inside 1.50 m. Ours keeps that stop zone and also uses how fast the worker is closing in, which phase of the task they are in, and a predictor that stops the robot if the worker would reach the stop zone within about 0.6 seconds at their current speed. The robot always gets the lowest of these speeds.

**Why a hidden Markov model and not deep learning?**
We had only 32 labelled training trials. A hidden Markov model trains on little data, its three phases are explainable, and it runs in real time. Because of the slow-only rule, a wrong phase can never make the robot faster.

**How accurate is the phase recognition?**
About 79% on held-out team trials and a median of 69% per participant on people it never saw. A phase error only changes the reduced-speed limit in the outer zone; it never removes a stop.

**How did you choose the zone sizes and thresholds?**
The 0.94 m stop radius comes from the ISO/TS 15066 separation formula: 0.4 s for the system to react and the robot to stop, at an approach speed of 1.6 m/s, plus 0.2 m for body parts the tracker misses and 0.1 m for sensor error. The outer zone is 1.6 times that, 1.50 m. The predictor's settings were chosen during development, partly in simulation to filter out noise, so all of these are settings for our lab cell, not measured values for an installed system.

**What happens if tracking is lost or a sensor fails?**
The robot stops. If the head position, body data or robot position is missing or out of date (for example, head tracking more than 150 ms old), every controller requests a stop before applying any of its own rules.

### How we tested it (Michael)

**Why two tracking systems?**
They do different jobs. The OptiTrack cameras locate markers on the participant's cap to about a millimetre in a fixed room frame, which gives a drift-free head position for distance and closing speed. The Xsens suit gives 23 body segments but drifts, so it supplied the whole-body distance in the later version; the two are not merged.

**What did participants actually do?**
Three blocks of the panel task, one controller per block, in one of six rotated orders. Each trial followed the real sequence: walk in and load the panel, step back, work under the raised panel, and step back while it is lowered. Each block had one plain trial, one with a brisk sideways movement and one with a brief step towards the robot, both practised first, then a short questionnaire.

**How did you keep participants safe?**
The study had Monash ethics approval (project 51642) and lab staff were present. Every controller shared the same stop zone and stopped the robot if tracking dropped out, so no block gave less protection than fixed zones. The panel was a lightweight stand-in, and the cued movements were short practised steps, not falls or lunges.

**What is same-input replay, and why trust it?**
We logged every sensor input, so we could run all three controllers on exactly the same recorded movement. The replay reproduced every command the live robot received, sample for sample. It shows what each controller would have asked for, not how the person would have moved differently, which we state as a limitation.

**Why only 12 participants?**
Each session took about 30 minutes of robot time with lab staff present, within one semester. Twelve is small, so we use the person as the unit of analysis and report effects that held for all 12, rather than claiming population-level results.

**Did the order of the blocks matter?**
For how people felt, yes: feeling relaxed rose from 3.78 to 4.67 out of 5 between the first and third block, whatever the controller, and the last block was most often picked as safest. Rotating the order spreads that effect across the controllers, and the robot measures avoid it altogether because the replay runs all three controllers on the same movement.

### The results (Michael)

**Is your controller safer than fixed zones?**
On the measured distance, all three controllers had already asked the robot to stop at every one of 970 entries, so they were equally safe there. Ours asked about a tenth of a second earlier for all 12 people, and that earlier request gives more margin when the distance is measured wrongly: 67% of entries still covered with a 5 cm error, against 31% for fixed zones.

**A tenth of a second sounds tiny. Does it matter?**
At the 1.6 m/s approach speed the zones are designed for, a tenth of a second is about 16 cm of walking. That head start is why our controller still covered 67% of entries with a 5 cm distance error, against 31% for fixed zones, and the advantage held at 10 cm too.

**Are the results statistically significant?**
We treat each person as one unit, because samples from the same person are not independent. Our controller stopped earlier than fixed zones for all 12 people, by 0.107 s on average (95% confidence interval 0.076 to 0.139 s; sign test p = 0.0005).

**Then why does the robot stop more with your controller?**
The intrusion predictor releases the stop as soon as the risk dips, so a noisy speed estimate makes it flicker between stop and go. That added under a second of unnecessary stopping per minute, and participants did not notice it. A short release delay should remove most of it.

**Did it slow the task down?**
No. Median trial times were 112.5 s with fixed zones, 113.6 s with reactive and 109.0 s with ours, although the robot spent a little more of that time stopped. We call efficiency comparable, not improved.

**How fast does the robot actually stop?**
A median of 0.16 seconds after the request and never more than 0.23 seconds. Adding the sensing and processing delays gives about 0.31 seconds in 95% of cases, so we recommend allowing 0.5 seconds rather than the 0.4 we assumed.

**Did the robot ever move close to anyone?**
Only while braking from a stop it had already been asked for; under normal motion the tool never came closer than 0.54 m. The close contact went the other way: five of the twelve people briefly pushed the held panel a few millimetres while working under it.

**Did participants feel safe?**
Yes, they rated all three controllers as safe and could not tell them apart: their "safest" picks were spread 3, 5 and 3, which is chance. Feeling relaxed rose with each block whatever the controller, which is a familiarity effect.

### Limitations and setbacks (Luke)

**Is this a certified safety system?**
No. It is a research prototype using the robot's ordinary speed controls, not a safety-rated stop. A site system would need safety-rated hardware and validation against ISO 10218 and ISO/TS 15066.

**What are the main limitations?**
The sample is small (12 people, recruited for convenience) and the software version changed halfway through. Some people shared an Xsens body profile, which adds up to about 5 cm of distance error, and the replay is open loop, so it cannot show how people would have moved under a different controller. It is also a lab task with cued movements, not a real site.

**What went wrong, and what would you do differently? (answer honestly)**
Midway through, the distance reference in the software changed, so the first six participants were measured to a fixed point rather than the moving panel. We analysed the two versions separately and say so in the paper. Next time we would freeze the software version before the first participant and log the reference point beside every distance.

### On site and next steps (Luke)

**Why is the robot stopped for a third of each trial?**
Because the task needs the worker inside the stop zone: to load the panel, work under it and unload it. People spent about 30% of their time inside the 0.94 m boundary under every controller, and almost all stopping was followed by the worker entering it within a second. So the limit is distance-based stopping during close work, not the choice of controller.

**What is a contact-safe mode?**
Our controllers keep the robot away from people, so it has to stop whenever the worker is close. A contact-safe mode, such as hand guiding or power and force limiting under ISO/TS 15066, lets a person work in contact with the robot safely. Our system already knows when the worker is under the panel, so it could switch into that mode there, which is where most of the stopping happens.

**Could this work on a real site without motion capture?**
Not as built: the cameras must be fixed around the cell and the suit calibrated for each person. The controller only needs a distance, a closing speed and body movement, so site-ready sensors could supply those, but we have not tested that, and a site system would also need safety-rated hardware.

**Could it be used for other tasks?**
The safety layer, meaning the distance limit and the stop when tracking drops out, does not depend on the task. Only the phase model does: a new task would need its phases defined and labelled training trials (we used 32). Because the model can only slow the robot, a weak model for a new task cannot make it less safe.

**What about cost and carbon?**
We did not measure either, so we make no claim. The extra costs are tracking equipment, calibration, maintenance and training; any carbon effect would need energy measurements compared with the manual process.

**What is new about your work, and who would use it?**
The novelty is the approach, not a new formula: the learned parts sit below a fixed distance limit, and we compared three controllers on exactly the same recorded human movement on a physical rig. It serves installation crews, contractors and the engineers who certify these systems, and it shows that task awareness can be added on top of a standard safety limit without weakening it.

**What would you do next?**
Add a short release delay to the predictor, allow 0.5 s for the robot to react, and measure each person's body profile fresh. Then switch to a contact-safe mode while the worker is under the panel, and test with real installation crews.
